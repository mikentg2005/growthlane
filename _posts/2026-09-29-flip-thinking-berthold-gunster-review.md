## The short version

Berthold Gunster is a Dutch theatre director and writer who founded the omdenken ("flip thinking") philosophy in 2001. *Flip Thinking: The Life-Changing Art of Turning Problems into Opportunities* is his best-known book, an international bestseller translated into more than twenty languages. It lays out fifteen strategies for turning a stuck, pessimistic problem into an opportunity, built on real, documented situations rather than hypothetical ones.

## The core idea: from "yes, but" to "yes, and"

Before Gunster even gets into the fifteen strategies, he spends time on acceptance, and this is the part that stuck with me most. His point is that a problem is really just the gap between what is actually happening and what you expected or wanted to happen. Camping in the rain is a disaster if you expected sunshine, and a relief if you're a farmer in a drought. The facts didn't change, only the expectation did.

From there, Gunster separates two very different moves. The first is "yes, but," the default reaction to a problem, where you accept it only far enough to immediately start resisting it. The second is "yes, and": accept reality exactly as it is, and *then* ask what you can do with it. He illustrates it simply: children dance in the rain, adults just try to avoid it. Chasing "what should be" keeps you stuck; asking "what could be" is what actually moves you forward. It's a small shift in language, but it reframes a problem as a fact you can act on instead of an injustice you have to fight.

## What's genuinely good about it

The fifteen strategies are each built around a real event, not a hypothetical one, and that turns out to matter more than it sounds. Reading a real situation naturally pulls you toward comparing it with something in your own life, so the book keeps nudging you to look at your own problems differently *while you're still reading it*, not just after you close it. That immediacy is the book's biggest strength: it doesn't ask you to wait until you finish to start applying anything.

It's also genuinely practical rather than purely motivational. Each strategy comes with steps toward actually practicing flip thinking, not just the idea that you should.

## The real downsides

The steps are there, but execution is uneven: some strategies land with a clear, repeatable method, and others feel thinner or more abstract than the strong ones. If you're hoping every one of the fifteen will hand you an equally concrete tool, a few will disappoint you.

The bigger honest caveat is one the book itself can't fully solve: how much this actually changes how you think depends on you, not just on reading it. Everyone who reads it seems to come away at least a little better at problem-solving, accepting what can't be changed, and seeing situations with more foresight, but the size of that shift depends entirely on whether you're willing to actually adopt the mindset instead of just nodding along.

## My take

I'd rate it 8/10 for how interesting it is and 7/10 for how practical it is. That gap is really the whole review: the ideas and the real-world examples are consistently engaging, but a few of the fifteen strategies could have been developed further before landing as fully as the "yes, and" chapter does. If you want a framework for reframing problems, backed by real situations instead of theory, it's worth the read. If you're after a purely feel-good pep talk, this isn't that, and if you're not willing to try applying anything, it won't do the work for you.
