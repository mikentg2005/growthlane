Leadership is not one skill either. Getting your first team, giving hard feedback, getting people to think for themselves and owning a mistake are different problems. The five books below each take one of them seriously, so this list is about matching the book to the situation you are in.

<div class="summary-box" markdown="1">
## In three sentences

These five books cover becoming a manager for the first time, giving honest feedback, asking better questions instead of giving answers, handing decisions to your team and taking responsibility for results. Start with the one that matches your situation now, not the most famous name. Reading will not make you a better leader by itself, so pick one idea and try it with your team this week.

**Who this list is not for:** anyone looking for a formula that works in every organization. These books are one author's experience told through stories, so treat each idea as something to test with your own team.
</div>

## Quick picks

- **If you have just become a manager:** *The Making of a Manager*
- **If you avoid giving honest feedback, or give it too bluntly:** *Radical Candor*
- **If you jump in with advice instead of asking questions:** *The Coaching Habit*
- **If you want your team to make more of the decisions:** *Turn the Ship Around!*
- **If you want to stop blaming circumstances and own the results:** *Extreme Ownership*

## At a glance

- ***The Making of a Manager*** (2019): a broad guide to the job. The easiest place to start.
- ***Radical Candor*** (revised edition 2019): one idea, care personally and challenge directly, applied to feedback.
- ***The Coaching Habit*** (2016): a short book built around seven questions to ask your team. 242 pages in the edition Kirkus listed.
- ***Turn the Ship Around!*** (2013): one true story about sharing decisions with a team.
- ***Extreme Ownership*** (2015): combat stories used to argue for taking full responsibility.

Each section below ends with one small thing to try this week. Those suggestions are mine, not the authors'.

## 1. The Making of a Manager, Julie Zhuo

{% include book-cover.html asin="0735219567" title="The Making of a Manager" %}

A guide for people who are managing others for the first time, written from the author's own experience of becoming a manager. It covers the practical basics of the job, such as what a manager is for, how to work with the people in your team and how to give feedback, in a plain and approachable style.

**Best for:** someone who has just been promoted, or is about to be, and wants a practical overview before reading more specialized books.

**Downside:** one reviewer found the advice clear but somewhat familiar, and a middle manager writing for an HR blog nearly dismissed it before finding a few useful ideas. If you have managed people for years, you may find little new.

**Try this week:** ask one person on your team what would make your next one-to-one more useful to them.

## 2. Radical Candor, Kim Scott

{% include book-cover.html asin="1250235375" title="Radical Candor" %}

Scott's idea is that a good boss cares personally and challenges directly at the same time. The book names four styles by combining those two things, and argues that most managers fail by being too nice to say what needs saying, or too harsh to be heard.

**Best for:** managers who avoid giving honest feedback, and managers who are blunt and want to stay kind.

**Downside:** one warning I found is that people can use the framework as an excuse to be mean. Scott's answer, according to a summary I read, is that candor is judged by the person who hears it, not the person who says it. One blog reviewer also felt the writing could have been better than the ideas deserved.

**Try this week:** pick one thing you have been holding back, say it kindly and directly, then ask how it landed.

## 3. The Coaching Habit, Michael Bungay Stanier

{% include book-cover.html asin="0978440749" title="The Coaching Habit" %}

The book argues that managers talk too much and ask too little. It gives seven questions to use in everyday conversations with your team, starting with what the author calls the Kickstart Question, so that you help people solve their problems instead of solving them for them. A Kirkus review (from its Indie program, which authors pay for) says it is short enough to finish in a couple of hours and that it cites supporting research.

**Best for:** managers who tend to jump in with advice, and anyone who wants a short, practical habit to try in conversations with their team.

**Downside:** the reviews I found were mostly favorable and I did not find a detailed critique or independent evidence on how well the method works. One reviewer said it is better thought of as a management book than a book about coaching, and another noted that the shift from telling to asking is difficult in practice.

**Try this week:** the next time someone brings you a problem, start with an open question such as "What's on your mind?" and hold back your advice for a minute.

<a href="https://www.amazon.de/dp/0978440749?tag=growthlane01-21" class="cta-button" rel="nofollow sponsored">Find it on Amazon</a>

## 4. Turn the Ship Around!, L. David Marquet

{% include book-cover.html asin="1591846404" title="Turn the Ship Around!" %}

Marquet tells the story of taking command of a US Navy submarine, the USS Santa Fe, at Pearl Harbor in 1999, and changing it from a team that waited for orders to one where people took the decisions themselves. He calls it moving from "leader-follower" to "leader-leader". The publisher's material says the submarine went from worst to first in its fleet, which is the author's side of the story.

**Best for:** anyone leading a team who wants to give people more authority and responsibility, and who prefers a true story to a list of tips.

**Downside:** it is one leader's account of one organization. One reviewer doubted that the approach carries over easily to groups that are not used to following orders, and another asked what you can do with it if you are not the leader.

**Try this week:** pick one decision you normally make yourself and ask your team how they would handle it.

<a href="https://www.amazon.de/dp/1591846404?tag=growthlane01-21" class="cta-button" rel="nofollow sponsored">Find it on Amazon</a>

## 5. Extreme Ownership, Jocko Willink and Leif Babin

{% include book-cover.html asin="1250183863" title="Extreme Ownership" %}

Two former Navy SEAL officers argue that a leader should take full responsibility for everything that happens in the team, including failures, and stop blaming people or circumstances. First published in 2015, the book builds its argument on stories from combat, including the 2006 battle of Ramadi, and from business.

**Best for:** leaders who catch themselves explaining failures with excuses, and readers who like direct stories from a military setting.

**Downside:** one reader review said it offers no groundbreaking new techniques, and one blogger argued that the claim that it works for every leader goes too far. Willink himself has said that some people use "extreme ownership" as a weapon against others instead of as a way to look at their own part, according to a podcast summary I read.

**Try this week:** after the next thing that goes wrong, write down your own part in it before you look at anyone else's.

<a href="https://www.amazon.de/dp/1250183863?tag=growthlane01-21" class="cta-button" rel="nofollow sponsored">Find it on Amazon</a>

## Also considered

Two other well-known titles did not make the list. *Leaders Eat Last* by Simon Sinek is popular, but critics say its explanations from neuroscience are simplified and that it reads more like a statement of values than an analysis. *Dare to Lead* by Brené Brown is highly rated, but some readers find it repetitive and overlapping with her earlier books. Both can still be worth reading, depending on what you want from a book.

## Where to start

If you have just become a manager, start with *The Making of a Manager*. If you already know your problem, go straight to the match: *Radical Candor* for feedback, *The Coaching Habit* for asking instead of telling, *Turn the Ship Around!* for sharing decisions and *Extreme Ownership* for responsibility.
